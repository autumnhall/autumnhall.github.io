Colonial archives in Spanish America contain many records documenting Indigenous histories, but many of these materials remain difficult to study even after digitization. In “Teaching Machines to Read the Conquest of America,” Patricia Murrieta-Flores discusses how machine learning could help researchers transcribe these manuscripts and search their contents. I was particularly interested in what this could mean for Indigenous communities, since some of these documents contain evidence of land claims that remain relevant today. Making these records easier to find could allow communities and researchers to access information about their own histories that has long required specialized archival training to recover.

Indigenous land titles demonstrate why access to colonial records is important for contemporary Indigenous communities. Murrieta-Flores et al. (2025) describe these documents as combining European writing conventions with Indigenous pictorial traditions. This combination shows how communities adapted colonial administrative practices for their own purposes. Some of the titles are still used in territorial disputes and religious contexts, and the authors call them “living documents” (4). Because the titles remain in use, the information in colonial archives is continually relevant to the descendants of the people who produced them, and making the records easier to find would benefit people outside academia as well.

Murrieta-Flores and her collaborators (2025) have developed handwritten text recognition (HTR) models that can transcribe sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Spanish manuscripts, with a character error rate as low as 5.25% for Redonda. In many archives, researchers have to rely on catalogue descriptions that offer limited information about a document’s contents, so their searches are shaped largely by human choice (3). A researcher studying a particular community could miss a document that mentions it simply because the community’s name does not appear in the catalogue entry. Searching the manuscripts themselves would reduce the risk of missing such documents, particularly in collections too large for researchers to read individually.

However, better transcription cannot resolve every difficulty. Even an accurate transcription of a Spanish land title would leave the Indigenous pictorial elements to be interpreted separately. Murrieta-Flores and Shanmugapriya (2026) make a related point when they write that “what counts as data is not given in advance” (2). A recognition model can only read the kinds of writing represented in its training materials, and technologies built around European languages may perform poorly on other writing traditions. As historians rely more on machine-generated transcriptions, I worry that the gaps already present in archival research might become harder to recognize.

There is also the question of ownership and continuity of these material holdings once they are fully digitized. Murrieta-Flores and Shanmugapriya (2026) discuss digitization projects carried out without sustained consultation with the communities whose cultural materials are being preserved. In such cases, a document can become publicly available while the people whose histories it records have little say in how it is described or circulated. Given the conditions under which colonial archives were originally produced, the consideration of who controls them today and how they are controlled is especially important.

I am optimistic about what Murrieta-Flores’s research could offer archival scholarship, and I would be particularly interested to see Indigenous communities use these tools to recover historical evidence that has long been inaccessible.
