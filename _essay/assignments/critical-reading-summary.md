 Our very first course session on defining digital humanities was also concerned with the kinds of work universities recognize as academic labor. Written between 2010 and 2017, these essays reflect a period when DH was becoming more formally established within the academy, prompting discussions about who could participate in the field and what qualified as digital scholarship. Although the authors approach these questions differently, their definitions frequently depend on distinctions between scholarly production and the work required to support it. Their discussions suggest that the emphasis on collaboration within digital humanities has developed alongside existing academic hierarchies, including those separating faculty scholarship from the technical work performed by librarians and other university staff.

Online publication offered new opportunities for scholars whose participation in conventional academic settings was limited. In “What Is Digital Humanities and What’s It Doing in English Departments?” Matthew Kirschenbaum discusses Brian Croxall’s experience at the 2009 MLA convention. After receiving no job interviews, Croxall could not justify the cost of attending, so he arranged for his paper to be read in his absence and posted it on his blog. According to Kirschenbaum, it became “by many orders of magnitude the most widely seen and read paper” from the convention (Kirschenbaum). The paper reached people who would never have heard it in the conference room, although Croxall himself remained excluded by the expense of attending. Kirschenbaum describes DH as “bound up with infrastructure,” acknowledging how much of the field’s growth depended on the systems supporting scholarly exchange (Kirschenbaum). Croxall’s experience also demonstrates how access to these new forms of participation remained shaped by the economic conditions of academic employment.

Disagreements over what counted as digital scholarship were also concerned with the work people actually performed. Stephen Ramsay’s declaration that “Digital Humanities is about building things” placed the creation of digital objects at the center of the field (Ramsay). His definition gave technical production an intellectual standing often denied to it in conventional humanities departments, although it also raised questions about whose building was recognized. Bethany Nowviskie approaches this distinction through the phrase “more hack; less yack,” originally a “goofball joke” at THATCamp about the format of academic conferences (Nowviskie). As the phrase circulated, it was increasingly interpreted as a preference for technical production over scholarly discussion. Nowviskie recalls the different demands that discussion placed on faculty and staff. Professors are often rewarded for exchanging ideas, while librarians and technologists may have to complete their assigned work after meetings end. As she explains, “too much yack and not enough hack in the working day makes us come in early and stay late” (Nowviskie). Her account complicates Ramsay’s emphasis on making by considering how universities classify the work involved. The same labor that qualifies someone to participate in digital humanities may still be considered a service to another scholar.

This distinction persists after a digital project has been published, when responsibility for its maintenance may pass to someone other than its original creator. In “Open Stacks: Making DH Labor Visible,” Laura Braunstein describes the expectation that libraries will preserve projects after the scholars responsible for them have retired. She imagines a faculty member asking the library to maintain a website “exactly the way it looks and feels today” while updating it “as technology changes” (Braunstein). Such a request assumes that preservation involves little additional work, when changes in software can require substantial technical decisions simply to keep a website functioning. Braunstein proposes “stack” alongside hack and yack to account for the infrastructure on which both activities depend. Her comparison with physical library stacks recalls how readers depend on systems of organization without usually considering the labor required to maintain them. She also notes that scholarship has long relied on “invisible (gendered) labor,” situating digital maintenance within a much older division of academic responsibilities (Braunstein). If building belongs within Ramsay’s definition of DH, the continuing work of preservation should be understood as part of that intellectual process.

These concerns were also pressing as technical training became associated with the possibility of employment beyond the conventional humanities job market. Wendy Hui Kyong Chun criticizes the practice of “rewriting political and pedagogical problems into technological ones,” including the suggestion that digital skills can resolve academic precarity (Chun et al.). Drawing on Lauren Berlant’s concept of “cruel optimism,” she examines how enthusiasm for DH can coexist with uncertain institutional support. Croxall’s widely circulated paper offers an example of this contradiction, since its success did little to change the circumstances that prevented him from attending MLA. Although these essays belong to an earlier period of digital humanities, the professional divisions they describe remain relevant to contemporary discussions of academic labor. Digital scholarship continues to depend on people whose contributions universities may classify as support, even when their work determines how research is produced and preserved.
