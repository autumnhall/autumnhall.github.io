I have a hard time with the word “agentic." Much of my research involves trying to understand the intellectual work women performed without receiving much recognition for it, so I find it strange that we are so willing to describe AI systems as independent agents. The language gives the technology a degree of authority that I am personally uncomfortable with, especially when its outputs depend on human decisions and existing bodies of knowledge. This was one of my main reservations going into Emily Johnson and Anastasia Salter’s lecture, “Critical (Un)Making and the Agentic Humanities.” I still have those reservations, although I found their approach to critical making more interesting than I expected.

In Critical Making in the Age of AI, Johnson and Salter discuss recovering agency through various practices of making and using research-creation as a means of creative thought and questioning. I think there is something useful about approaching AI through experimentation, even if you are skeptical of the technology itself. I can question whether generative AI is capable of interpreting historical materials, but what happens when I actually ask it to do so? What kinds of mistakes does it make, and what might those mistakes reveal about how historical knowledge is represented?

One experiment I would like to try involves women's correspondence. Through my work on women's labor of the long eighteenth century, I have become interested in the difficulties of reconstructing women's intellectual contributions from incomplete records. I would like to play around with this by taking a piece of correspondence involving editorial advice and ask a generative AI system to explain the relationship between the people involved. I might then provide additional archival context and see whether its interpretation changes. Would it recognize a woman's editorial suggestions as intellectual contributions? Would it acknowledge uncertainty about the relationship, or attempt to provide a complete explanation despite the missing evidence?

I think comparing the two responses would be interesting, particularly because so much of my own research involves questioning the assumptions that have shaped how women's work is remembered. There is also a problem with the experiment itself. If I select the documents and supply the additional context, how much of the resulting interpretation is actually coming from the system? I would want to consider that question alongside the responses, since the decisions I make about what information to provide will inevitably influence what the system produces.

Johnson and Salter acknowledge that “the rise of AI has panicked many in education," and I appreciate their willingness to consider how scholars and teachers might respond to these technologies. I am still skeptical of their growing presence in academic research, especially when a convincing response can conceal substantial gaps in the evidence. However, I think critical making offers an interesting way to investigate those concerns. I would like to experiment with AI in ways that allow me to question its interpretations and understand where they come from. I remain unconvinced that agency is the right term for these systems, and while I am unsure what exactly the replacement might be, I think examining how much human work goes into producing their responses would be a useful place to begin.
