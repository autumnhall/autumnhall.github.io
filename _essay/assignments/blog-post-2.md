My research concerns women’s participation in eighteenth-century scientific culture, with a focus on the knowledge involved in cosmetic preparation. I began thinking more carefully about how that knowledge survives in the archive while working on the Digital Humanities projects the Maria Edgeworth Letters Project and the Collective Biographies of Women. Preparing correspondence for a digital edition involved many, many decisions about dating letters and identifying people mentioned in them, sometimes with limited information to work from. I became interested in how those decisions affected the account of women’s lives and relationships that readers could reconstruct. I think that experience also made me more conscious of the editorial labor behind the sources I use in my own research.

My current thesis project examines puppy water, a cosmetic preparation recorded in Mary Doggett’s receipt book and mentioned in Jonathan Swift’s satirical poem “The Lady’s Dressing Room.” The difference between these accounts interests me greatly. Doggett records a method of preparation, treating the substance as something to be made and used. Swift places it among the objects that disgust Strephon as he searches Celia’s dressing room. I want to understand how a preparation associated with practical knowledge came to appear in this kind of satire, and whether other contemporary writing treated women’s cosmetic practices in similar ways.

I would like to incorporate Omeka S into my research to organize a collection of manuscript recipes and printed discussions of cosmetics. The sources I need are spread across different archives and editions, which makes it difficult to follow references from one text to another. By consolidating these items into one location, I could improve accessibility and better compare how preparations were described while keeping track of where each account originated. My work on the Edgeworth letters has taught me to be careful with incomplete dates and uncertain attributions, since the way we organize documents can influence how others interpret them.

I think OpenRefine would also be useful for organizing this material. The information that I gather from catalogues is rarely consistent, and I could use it to reconcile records while checking those changes against the original descriptions. I think this is an important part of the process because decisions about which entries belong together will also affect what future users of my research are able to access. I would also like to experiment with Voyant to examine patterns in how cosmetic labor was described. How does the language of a recipe compare with discussions of similar practices in scientific writing or satire? I think text analysis would help me identify recurring terms and connections across a larger collection, although I would still need to examine individual passages through close reading.

Digital methods are useful for my research because they allow me to examine women’s scientific work across documents that are rarely considered together. I also want to remain attentive to the gaps in the surviving evidence and how those gaps influence what we can conclude. By using DH in my research, I hope to better understand how women’s domestic knowledge circulated within eighteenth-century scientific culture and how its treatment changed across different forms of writing.
