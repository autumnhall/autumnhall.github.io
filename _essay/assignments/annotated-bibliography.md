Early Modern Recipes Online Collective (EMROC). University of Essex, EMROC Project.

The Early Modern Recipes Online Collective is the closest methodological model for the collection I want to develop. Its work on transcribing and connecting manuscript recipe books from approximately 1550 to 1800 shows how documents held in different repositories can be consolidated into an accessible, digitized research resource. I am interested in its emphasis on searching by ingredient and process, since those categories would help me in my research on cosmetic preparations across manuscripts. The collaborative transcription process is also worth considering, as I’ve done similar work before using Zooniverse. This searchable recipe collection is largely dependent on editorial decisions about handwriting and terminology, and I to look at as many examples of this as possible before drafting my own. 

The Recipes Project. The Recipes Project, Hypotheses.

I chose The Recipes Project because it brings together research on historical recipes across a variety of disciplines. Its broad historical scope and interdisciplinary approach provide useful examples of how scholars might interpret recipes differently within different historical contexts. I also appreciate how its essays connect individual recipes to broader scholarly discussions, which is something I hope to accomplish in my own research. However, I have some concerns about the website’s accessibility and how easily readers can navigate its materials. I would like to consider these limitations when developing my own digital collection, using the project as a model for what I hope to accomplish and what I would like to avoid.

Batchelor, Jennie, Koenraad Claes, and Jenny DiPlacidi. The Lady's Magazine: Understanding the Emergence of a Genre—Index. University of Kent.

The annotated index to The Lady's Magazine covers more than fifteen thousand items published between 1770 and 1818. I chose it because women's periodicals offer another place to investigate how knowledge about the body circulated beyond manuscript recipe books. The index provides information about individual items and their authors or sources, which makes it useful for tracing topics across individual issues that would otherwise be difficult to search. I am also interested in the project's response to unreliable text recognition and misleading original titles. Its editors also show why careful indexing remains necessary even after historical women's periodicals have been digitized.

Women Writers Project. Women Writers Online. Northeastern University.

Women Writers Online makes early women's printed writing available through a collection of encoded transcriptions. Although my research also uses manuscripts, I chose this project because it offers a model for treating women's writing as a substantial, varied body of evidence. The use of text encoding raises questions I would have to consider when creating a digital collection: which features of a document should be marked, and what will readers be able to search? Its accompanying metadata and scholarly resources are useful examples of how an edition can support research without separating a text from its publication history.

Gale. Eighteenth Century Collections Online (ECCO).

Eighteenth Century Collections Online (ECCO) is a searchable collection of printed books, pamphlets, and broadsides from 1701 to 1800. I would use it to search for references to cosmetics in scientific writing and literary texts that I might not find through a search for individual authors. I could compare these references with manuscript recipes to better understand how domestic preparations appeared in print. ECCO also raises methodological concerns because the text searches depend on the quality of textual recognition software and historical spelling.

Leong, Elaine. Recipes and Everyday Knowledge: Medicine, Science, and the Household in Early Modern England. University of Chicago Press, 2018. 

Elaine Leong's study provides a historical framework for understanding recipe books as records of knowledge production. She shows how householders collected, tested, exchanged, and revised recipes, placing these activities within the history of early modern science. I chose the book because it will help me to learn more about the actual experimentation and practical expertise behind early modern science. It also offers a way to think about circulation of recipe books as well, as a recipe copied into a notebook might have passed through several hands before reaching its form. I want to keep those exchanges in mind when comparing manuscript instructions with printed accounts of similar preparations.

Burke, Jill, and Wilson Poon. “‘Your Companions Will Teach You’: Makers' Knowledge in Renaissance Cosmetics Recipes.” Embodied Experiences of Making in Early Modern Europe: Bodies, Gender, and Material Culture, edited by Sarah Bendall and Serena Dyer, Amsterdam University Press, 2025, pp. 109–134. https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.22836051?turn_away=true 

Jill Burke and Wilson Poon examine Renaissance cosmetic recipes as records of practical knowledge, including skills that written instructions alone could not communicate. Their discussion of recipe annotations and the reconstruction of an anti-wrinkle preparation interests me because it addresses the limits of treating a recipe as self-explanatory text. My research uses later materials, but the methodological problem is similar. How much can historians learn about preparation from written directions, and what knowledge would have been passed on in practice? I chose this chapter to think more carefully about the relationship between recorded instructions and the work of making cosmetics.

Pierazzo, Elena. Digital Scholarly Editing: Theories, Models and Methods. Routledge, 2020. 

Pierazzo examines how historical documents are prepared for digital publication, including the decisions editors make about transcription and how to represent features of the original manuscripts. I chose Pierazzo’s book because I think it will be useful as I begin developing my own digital collection. Many of the historical documents I work with have uncertain dates or authorship, and even something as simple as a name can appear differently across sources. I am interested in how digital editors approach these problems and make decisions about what information to include. Pierazzo’s discussion also made me think about the difference between digitizing a manuscript and preparing an edition that other researchers can use. I want to make my sources more accessible while being careful about how my editorial decisions might affect their interpretation.

D'Ignazio, Catherine, and Lauren F. Klein. Data Feminism. MIT Press, 2020. 

D'Ignazio and Klein argue that data practices reflect relationships of power, including decisions about whose experiences are represented and how information is categorized. This is useful for my research because women's domestic scientific work can be difficult to identify through the categories used in historical catalogues. I want to consider how a digital collection might describe cosmetic preparation without reproducing assumptions that treat domestic labor as separate from scientific knowledge. The book also encourages me to question the selections that might impact datasets.
